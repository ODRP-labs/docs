A leg to stand on for Reticulum

Reticulum is the right idea.
It just can't reach yet.

Unstoppable, off-grid, no service provider, no permission — that's the promise. Today it reaches a few neighbors trading text across town. Long Haul gives it the legs to matter: real range, on HF, and legal right now.

01 — The gap

A beautiful idea, stuck at neighborhood scale.

Reticulum is the best thing going in resilient networking — real routing, self-healing paths, no carrier, no kill-switch. Its own stated vision is to let anyone build networks that cover vast areas with no infrastructure. But one leg is missing: reach. LoRa and Wi-Fi HaLow top out at line-of-sight and a few miles. The only infrastructure-free band that jumps the horizon — across a county, a country, an ocean — is HF.

And that's the wall. Reticulum's encryption is mandatory — there's no switch to turn it off — and FCC Part 97 (§97.113) bans encryption on the air. So the one network built for grid-down legally can't touch the one band that still reaches when the grid is down. It stays a neighborhood toy.

02 — The fix

Long Haul gives it that leg — without breaking a thing.

Long Haul does not turn Reticulum's encryption off. You can't, and it doesn't try. Every mesh stays fully encrypted; the only place data rides in the clear is the single HF hop between meshes. Long Haul is an application-layer gateway: it runs as a legitimate node that stands in for the far side's nodes, so it is the real addressed recipient. It decrypts what's sent to it because it holds the keys, carries the plaintext across one HF hop in the clear (legal), and the gateway on the far side re-injects it into that mesh, addressed to the real node. What it can't do is cryptographically prove you sent it: across the bridge, the far node trusts the gateway operators to vouch for identity, not an end-to-end signature.

It doesn't fight Reticulum's security model — it uses the one thing no encryption can stop: a legitimate recipient choosing to republish what it received. It doesn't disable the encryption inside either mesh — it carries the one cross-site hop in the clear. That's the trade, and it gives Reticulum legs.

03 — Straight talk

What it gives up, and where it stands.

The trade

The HF hop itself is cleartext. Anyone on that frequency can read it — that's what makes it legal, not a bug. Each local mesh keeps its full encryption; only the long-haul hop is open. Picture the two meshes as one network and the encryption simply isn't there in the middle — that's the deliberate cost, not a detail I tuck away. Perfect for EMCOMM ("we're OK, need water at 50th and Broadway"). Wrong for anything private — and nothing gets you private over ham HF anyway.

Where it stands

The whole bridge works end-to-end in simulation — both directions, files, two modem families, the signed & paired air leg, even the encrypted adversarial flip. Proven on a real modem over a virtual sound card. Not yet on the air. A working, sim-proven core and a build plan — not a product for sale.

04 — When it counts

And the switch is already there.

When there's no one left enforcing the rules — a grid-down day with no FCC left to answer to — you flip to adversarial mode: one switch drops Long Haul out of the path and runs full-encryption Reticulum straight over the radio. You don't build it that day. You already trained on the hardware. You already have it standing.

05 — The other answers

Everyone else's answer to the same wall.

Ask the crowd that wants this how they'd get Reticulum onto HF. You'll hear a handful of answers. Most don't survive contact with the day you'd actually need them.

Answer 1 — Run it illegally

"Screw the FCC, run it anyway."

Three groups hide in here. The talkers — all hat, no cattle. They'll tell you they do whatever they want, but they've never actually run Reticulum over HF. They're just here to argue.

The one-off testers — proved a link in the woods once, tore it down before anyone with a badge showed up; respect, but a bench test with extra steps. Proving a link on a good afternoon isn't proving a station. You still don't know what it does at midnight, in the rain, at 110° or 10°, or two hours into a real traffic load.

The full-timers — basically nobody: hiding a solar-powered illegal node on public land is the most gear, the most money, and still one guy with fox-hunting gear away from being seized.

Answer 2 — Give up on HF

"Reticulum's a LoRa mesh; it was never meant for HF."

Except a mesh with no long leg never covers anything real. You'd need everyone carrying a node — and the same country that went to the moon nearly sixty years ago still has cell-phone dead zones. A mesh stood up by individuals with LoRa nodes isn't reaching coast to coast, not in our lifetimes. And the day an "Internet 2.0" dented a carrier's margin, they'd go to Washington and lobby LoRa into the grave. "It's a national security risk" — some guy that works for a service provider, probably. Reticulum rides any medium by design; HF is just the long one.

Answer 3 — Wait for the day

"I'll figure it out when it happens."

These are the same guys who bought a Baofeng off Amazon and threw it in a drawer, figuring they'll work it out the day the apocalypse starts. You don't learn your rig during the emergency. Learning it beforehand is the entire point.

Answer 4 — Push to change the law

"Don't change Reticulum — change the law."

Reticulum's creator has said the software won't change; the rules should. Push the FCC to allow encryption on the ham bands, then run Reticulum over the air as-is.

But the same government using your tax dollars to fund Palantir and Flock isn't about to turn pro-encryption at the agency across the road. Our government is trending toward more surveillance, not less — so encryption on the ham bands is a fairy tale. As a matter of fact, I wouldn't be surprised if Wi-Fi HaLow and LoRa end up a licensed service at some point, because the government can't have us peasants running around setting up encrypted networks they can't surveil.

Everyone here is inside the rules. Long Haul is the only bet that needs nothing from Washington to keep working.
06 — The real objection

"Running Reticulum unencrypted defeats the entire purpose."

That's the objection underneath every other objection, and it deserves a real answer. Here it is: Long Haul is a test bed. A training range.

If I want to train firearm skills, I train at a gun range — safely, contained, and legally. I still train with my real equipment. I build the muscle memory. I learn what my gear can actually do, and at what ranges. Politics aside, a gun is designed to kill things — but I don't go out and kill things to train. That would be illegal and inhumane. So I use a training platform: the range.

It's the same with Reticulum. I can't run the full stack over HF, because the full stack is encrypted. What I can do is isolate the key parts of the communications stack: run full, real Reticulum networks on both sides, and put a legal cleartext HF leg in the middle.

That lets you train on the real thing — real traffic volume, real node establishment, real data transfers, real power consumption, real propagation, real environmental conditions — over an actual span of time, not a single afternoon.

It's a test bed. It lets you test yourself and your equipment now, so that if the day comes, you're not scrambling to figure it out.

There's a simpler answer, too. Designers design products, and products are almost never used exactly the way the designer intended. Anybody who's ever been four hours into a thirty-minute project and needed to hit something — with nothing in hand but a crescent wrench — knows the moment the crescent wrench became an adjustable hammer. It's a joke, but it's the point: the end user decides what a tool is for, not the designer, and "it wasn't designed for that" has never once stopped anyone. There are only two kinds of people who have never used a wrench as a hammer — liars, and people who've never picked up a wrench.

07 — Bigger than the fight

And maybe you don't care about the encryption fight at all.

Maybe you just need a resilient network with real range — disaster relief, humanitarian comms, an off-grid community, a region the carriers skipped. Set the whole encryption argument aside and Reticulum is still superb. Long Haul just lets it reach, legally.

Long Haul is whatever legal, long-range, infrastructure-free networking you need it to be.
