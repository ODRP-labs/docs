Cleartext Reticulum bridge · early R&D
LONG HAUL

A fully encrypted Reticulum mesh at each site — bridged over ham radio, legally, by making only the over-the-air hop cleartext.

Reticulum is a genuinely good post-internet mesh. Its one dealbreaker for ham radio is that it encrypts everything, and encryption is illegal on the amateur bands. Long Haul is the way around that: each mesh stays fully encrypted, and only the hop between them crosses in the clear — the one part the law is actually about.

The wall everyone hits

Three reasons "Reticulum over HF" keeps dead-ending

97.113

Part 97 bans encryption on the ham bands

US amateur rules prohibit transmitting messages "encoded for the purpose of obscuring their meaning." Encryption does exactly that. So encrypted traffic on the amateur bands isn't a grey area — it's flatly not allowed.

by design

Reticulum is encrypted, always — and that won't change

There is no switch to turn it off; encryption is baked into the protocol. Reticulum's creator has said plainly that unencrypted operation will never be natively supported — the position is that the regulations should change, not the software.

Sure. And have you ever been to a ham club meeting? That policy change is not coming. We need an answer that works with the rules as they are.

no relay

You can't just forward the traffic, either

The obvious workaround — a middleman that relays packets — doesn't work. If you aren't the destination node, Reticulum never exposes the content to you. A relay sees ciphertext it has no keys for. There is nothing to decrypt in transit.

The approach

Don't intercept it. Become the endpoint.

Long Haul never disables a mesh's encryption and never reads someone else's traffic. It becomes a legitimate place to send yours — then carries that one cross-site hop in the clear.

01

For every node on the far side, Long Haul stands up a real, addressable stand-in on your local mesh — a puppet. You point traffic at it exactly like any other contact.

02

Because that puppet is a genuine Reticulum destination Long Haul owns, it receives and decrypts your message legitimately — as the endpoint, not as a snoop. No keys stolen, nothing forged.

03

It hands the plaintext to your modem and sends it over the radio in the clear — the one leg the law actually cares about.

04

A Long Haul station on the far end re-encrypts it and injects it back into that mesh, addressed to the real node. Every local mesh stays normal, fully-encrypted Reticulum — the clear leg is only the hop between them.

The honest answer

“Becoming the endpoint” isn't spoofing — and it isn't a backdoor

If a gateway decrypts your traffic, the fair question is: doesn't it hold your keys, stand in for you, and open a hole someone else could walk through? Here's the mechanical answer, point by point.

→

Your private keys never leave your node. The gateway never has them and never asks for them. Nothing is handed over, nothing is stolen, nothing is forged.

→

A puppet is a separate node — not a copy of yours. Every Reticulum destination has two different “names”: a destination hash, its real address, derived from its own key pair and impossible to copy or forge; and a plain display label — the handle you'd recognize a contact by — that any node can set. A puppet is a brand-new destination with its own keys and its own hash. The only thing it borrows from the far-side node is that human-readable handle — so you recognize it.

→

Decrypting your message is delivery, not interception. Reticulum routes on the hash, never the handle. When you address a puppet, that puppet genuinely is the endpoint — it decrypts its own mail exactly like any node does. It cannot read traffic addressed to the real far-side node (different hash, different keys), and it can't touch anything outside its own puppets. It is not a general decryptor.

→

The radio hop carries no Reticulum. Each gateway ends one encrypted network and re-originates into the other — a store-and-forward bridge between two healthy, fully-encrypted meshes. Routing never crosses the cleartext leg, so no mesh has its encryption disabled. What is exposed, plainly, is the data on that middle hop: it crosses in the clear. That's the trade, stated out loud, not a hidden cost.

→

You opt in, on your own hardware. Both gateways belong to your group, run by people you already trust. No one's traffic is exposed unless they choose to address a puppet. The genuinely new exposure is the HF hop in the clear — the content, and to any listener the traffic pattern around it: which handle is talking to which handle, when, and how much. (Handles are pseudonyms; the callsign that ties it to a real operator is on the air in any mode anyway.) That's the legal requirement, and the cost every ham already accepts on the air.

→

Watching both legs doesn't unravel it. The mesh legs use ephemeral keys — per-link for established Links (Curve25519 ECDH), per-packet for one-off sends — with AES-256 and forward secrecy, and the two sides are different sessions — so seeing the ciphertext on one side and the cleartext on the HF leg hands no one your keys.

→

What you don't get: end-to-end proof of sender. Native Reticulum lets a recipient verify exactly who sent a message; across a Long Haul bridge that chain is broken — the far node sees identity vouched for by the gateway operators, not signed by the real you. In a closed group that already trusts its gateways that's an accepted trade — but it's a trade, and I name it.

The short version: a puppet is a real, legitimate destination that happens to wear a familiar name — so decrypting what's addressed to it is ordinary delivery, on hardware you chose, and no key ever changes hands.

Built to be flexible

Your modem, your band, your mission

Modem-agnostic

Two modes per link: ARQ — acknowledged, retried, confirmed delivery (recommended for anything that has to arrive intact) — or fire-and-forget for broadcast and low-latency tactical traffic.

More than text

Carries the whole payload

Images, files, position/telemetry, and remote commands — not just chat. On HF you size it to the band; on faster links, more.

Real routing

Address the actual node

You reach specific far-side nodes, not one dumb gateway. Routing survives the hop — even nodes sitting behind a relay.

Guard rails

Per-link data limits proven in sim

The gateway paces its writes to the modem's own queue so nothing overruns it, and each link has a data cap sized to the modem. Address a puppet with something oversized and it bounces back — on your own mesh, before the radio ever keys — with the real numbers. You set the cap per link; nobody shoves a 1 GB file across an HF leg. (Proven in sim; not yet on a radio rig.)

Control

Remote command & control proven in sim

An unattended gateway you can't walk to, you manage by messaging the Long Haul node itself — status, arm pairing, set the data cap, switch modem, restart — gated to authorized local users, with an audit trail. You can also drive it from a point-and-click control page any Reticulum browser — MeshChat, Sideband, NomadNet — renders, so there's nothing to memorize, identity-gated to a whitelisted set of actions. And it reaches across the hop too: the settings the two paired gateways share — modem/band, data cap, and operating frequency — now change on both at once through a signed handshake with auto-revert (if the new setting kills the link, each side rolls itself back to what worked). All proven in sim. Still ahead: a read-only peek at the far gateway's status, and 2G ALE to auto-pick the working HF frequency.

Common questions

A few things that come up

So does it break Reticulum's encryption, or not?

Both answers are true, and the honest one is both at once. Each mesh is unchanged — normal, fully-encrypted Reticulum, routing intact. But the hop from one mesh to the other is cleartext, so that data is not encrypted on the radio leg. Picture the two meshes as one network and the encryption simply isn't there in the middle. That's the deliberate trade that makes it legal — and it's exactly why this only fits traffic you're willing to send in the open. What Long Haul never does is switch off encryption inside a mesh or force its way in; what it does is carry the one cross-site hop in the clear.

Why not just test the link with VARA or plain Mercury?

That proves the RF path — propagation, power, antenna — in a snapshot, and it's worth doing. What it doesn't prove is the workload over time. A single connect-exchange-disconnect tells you nothing about the sustained, real-world traffic you'd see on a Reticulum network — multi-node message flow, file backlogs, retries, roster/presence sync — carried across hours of shifting conditions: the day/night cycle, changing weather and ionospheric propagation, the duty cycle and solar draw of staying up, and (eventually) the ALE layer picking your working frequency. That's what Long Haul puts over a real modem under real conditions. (The Reticulum protocol itself runs inside each mesh, not on the HF leg — so this tests the link and the load, not native Reticulum-over-HF, which is the day-of mode.)

Can the gateway read any Reticulum traffic, or only mine?

Only traffic addressed to the puppets it hosts — the endpoints on the far side of the leg that you're actually addressing. It holds no other keys, so it can't read anything else and never touches it. It is not a general Reticulum decryptor, and couldn't be.

Doesn't cleartext on the air kill anonymity?

Long Haul is built for a closed group who already know each other, so member-to-member anonymity was never the goal — a hash wasn't hiding your identity from people who already know your node. Over the air, messages are addressed by your handle — your label or display name — not by the routing hash, and a handle is a pseudonym you pick: “Purple Bears” is no more you than any other handle. So an outside listener sees the pattern between handles — which handle is talking to which handle, when, and how much — not identities. The one thing that ties a transmission to a real person is the operator's callsign, which Part 97 puts on the air in any mode, Long Haul or not. Outsider anonymity was never the ham-radio promise; legality and reach are what this buys. And every puppet announces its name with a trailing “ (P)”, so anyone browsing sees at a glance it's a Long Haul puppet whose traffic is cleartext at the radio hop.

Do the two gateways talk Reticulum to each other over the radio?

No. Over the air it's a plain framed byte pipe between the two gateways — acknowledgement and retry there are the modem's job (Mercury ARQ, AX.25 connected mode). Reticulum lives on each side only; the gateway terminates it before the radio and re-originates it after.

What actually travels in the clear on the HF leg?

The payloads you addressed to puppets, plus the node presence and handles Long Haul needs to know which puppets to stand up. Nothing else. Everything inside each local mesh stays normal, fully-encrypted Reticulum.

Is it Part 97 compliant beyond the encryption rule?

Yes, by design. The over-the-air data is cleartext (§97.113); station ID is built in, not bolted on — the operator's callsign goes on the air as plain text (DE <call> appended to every Mercury frame; the source-address field on AX.25), satisfying §97.119, and a gateway with no callsign set refuses to key. The mesh's users ride as third-party traffic (§97.115) under that licensed operator. And the on-air format is published — the message content is plaintext and the exact byte layout is public, down to the fragment header and the signed envelope. The only cryptography is an appended Ed25519 signature that authenticates the sending station without hiding a thing — the opposite of a code “intended to obscure.”

Does everyone on the mesh need a ham license?

No — only the two HF station operators do. Everyone else on a mesh can be unlicensed: the licensed operator carries their messages as third-party traffic (§97.115), the same rule that lets a licensed ham relay a non-ham's message. So a whole group can run their Reticulum handles behind one or two licensed gateways — only the folks keying the HF radios need a ticket.

Does Long Haul do mesh networking or multi-hop routing across many sites?

Not yet — today it's a point-to-point bridge between two sites. Each site can be a full, normal Reticulum mesh with as many local nodes as you like — that part's supported and proven — but the Long Haul link itself joins exactly two gateways over one HF hop. Linking three or more sites, or relaying across several HF hops (a mesh of gateways), is a later feature. The discipline is simple: get two-site point-to-point rock-solid and proven on real radios first, then extend.

“Encryption is gravity” — isn't this just not what Reticulum was made for?

It's a fair challenge, and it usually arrives with the Zen of Reticulum's “Encryption Is Not A Feature” — the point that encryption is structural rather than a bolt-on, and that pulling it out would break routing itself. That's exactly correct, and Long Haul is built around it, not against it.

The distinction that dissolves the objection: Long Haul never strips encryption out of Reticulum. Every mesh runs full, unmodified Reticulum — routing, signatures, path validation, all intact. The cleartext leg isn't Reticulum at all: each gateway terminates one encrypted Reticulum network and re-originates into the other, with a plain framed byte pipe over the radio in between. Reticulum's internals never run in the clear, so the exact failure the Zen warns about — routing breaking because packets went plaintext — never happens. The honest other half: seen as one end-to-end path, your data does cross that middle hop in the clear. That's the deliberate, legal trade, and the reason this only fits traffic you'd send in the open.

Some will still say this isn't what Reticulum was made for — not what the designer intended. But designers rarely get to dictate what a product ends up being used for. Masonry bricks become wheel chocks; old gym equipment becomes a clothes rack; anyone who's ever held a wrench has used it as a hammer; the chainsaw was first designed for surgery during childbirth. The person who decides what a tool is for is the end user, not the designer.

Beyond that, Long Haul is a test bed. An automaker doesn't rate a car's safety by sending a stunt driver backwards up I-95 at rush hour to crash into people — that's illegal and reckless. They use a controlled environment: a crash-test dummy, a barrier, sixty into a wall behind bulletproof glass. Long Haul is that. It isn't a final answer; it's a way to take Reticulum and test it in a controlled, legal, repeatable environment.

Take a car off the road — did you lose the point of the car? Take encryption off Reticulum — did you lose the point of Reticulum? Depends which point you mean. The whole point of a car is to move people; the whole point of Reticulum is to be an encrypted network. And you can't test an encrypted network over HF — so yes, I pulled the encryption off the HF leg, so I could test everything else: the radio, the duty cycle, the solar setup it takes to hold 100% uptime, data-transfer rates, signal-to-noise ratios, message-backlog handling, RF propagation — everything you can't test with plain ham radio, plain LoRa, or plain Reticulum.

Is it a compromise? Maybe — the same way putting a car in a crash-test rig is a compromise, if that's how you want to word it. I'm not here to reinvent Reticulum, and I'm not here to rip the encryption out of it. I'm building a way for you to practice using your system before it's too late.

A last word: some of you may read all this and conclude I don't respect Mark's work or his design. It's the opposite. I'm genuinely impressed by Reticulum and invested in the project — the only shortcoming I can see is that today there's no legitimate way to test it on HF, and Long Haul is my attempt to fill that gap with an isolated test bed: to prove the system, as designed, is as robust and undefeatable as I believe it is.

Where it is right now

Proven in simulation. Ready for real radios.

The full stack already works together on the bench — real Reticulum nodes, a real Mercury HF modem and a real Direwolf AX.25 modem, bridged over a virtual sound card on a radio-like shared medium (a keyed side hears nothing). A message crosses the entire chain and lands at the right node:

Bob→encrypted mesh→Long Haul→Mercury→radio (cleartext)→Mercury→Long Haul→encrypted mesh→Sue
  • ✓Full flow over Mercury ARQ. Bob reaches an isolated Sue across the whole chain — 102 s, zero retries. Both directions at once, queued together: both delivered in 84 s.
  • ✓Files, not just text. A 3 KB image, a file attachment and a telemetry blob arrived byte-exact (135 s). Bigger: 20 KB in 327 s, 50 KB in 690 s — roughly 100 bytes/s on a clean simulated channel. Text and telemetry are seconds; a 200 KB photo is about 35 minutes.
  • ✓Noisy channel. Both-ways text at ~2 dB SNR delivered with zero retries (Mercury dropped itself into its robust mode). At ~−3 dB the full image + file + telemetry payload still arrived byte-exact, with a real lost frame resent, in about 20 minutes. It keeps completing down to −7 dB and gives out near −8.6 dB, where the connect handshake itself can't finish — that's Mercury's own rated floor, so Long Haul adds no penalty of its own.
  • ✓Second modem family. AX.25 connected mode on VHF/UHF via Direwolf: text in 18 s; both ways plus the same image + file + telemetry byte-exact in 117 s, Direwolf's own retransmissions covering a few lost frames. Same gateway, same flows.
  • ✓Nodes self-announce; Long Haul auto-builds puppets and tears them down when a node leaves. Even nodes sitting behind a relay still work.
  • ✓Since then, all proven in sim: gateway signatures + PIN pairing (the air leg is authenticated), first-come handle-collision handling, per-link data caps with a bounce-back, the config UI + a native tab in the station app, and a Long Haul control node you can message for status and near-side command & control.
  • ✓And most recently (all sim): adversarial mode now delivers (encrypted Reticulum straight over Mercury, ciphertext-only on the air); coordinated far-side control (retune both gateways together, auto-revert); browsing NomadNet pages & files over the radio, legally; an encrypted-content guard that refuses to put already-encrypted data on the air; CAT control of the radio's own dial; and the ability to join an existing Reticulum hub so a gateway's nodes show up in MeshChat or Sideband.
  • →Still to build: the other modem drivers (VARA HF/FM, ARDOP), leaner turns over ARQ (coalescing small frames), a live "frame 3 of 5, 62 %" transfer readout, and keep-calling on a marginal channel.
  • →Every number above is simulation; an on-air floor will differ. Needs a current upstream Mercury build — the packaged 1.9.13 deadlocks two-way ARQ. The big next step: off the bench and onto real radios, between real operators.
